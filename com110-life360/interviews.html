Their Voices

Edited interview highlights from both of my daughters

I wanted this page to feel more personal because one of the most important parts of this project was not only researching what scholars say, but also listening to the teenagers in my own home. These answers are organized and lightly edited for clarity, but they preserve the main ideas from each conversation.

Interview 1: Age 15 Interview 2: Age 13 Topic: Life360, trust, safety, independence

01 — Interview note

I did not want to talk about teenagers without hearing from them directly.

For this project, I interviewed both of my daughters separately. I chose not to use their names, schools, routes, or any identifying details. I only refer to them by age. That was important to me because the project is public-facing, and I wanted to respect their privacy while still including their real perspectives.

Something I appreciated about both interviews is that neither girl gave me a simple black-and-white answer. Both of them understood the safety side of Life360, but both also recognized that there is a line between using an app for protection and using it in a way that feels excessive. That honesty made this page one of the most meaningful parts of the project for me.

02 — Interview 1

Older daughter (age 15)

Age 15

How do you feel knowing I can see your location?

She said she mostly feels okay with it because she understands that it helps me know where she is if something happens, but she also admitted that it can feel strange knowing someone can always locate you.

Does Life360 make you feel safer, watched, or both?

Her answer was clear: a little bit of both. She said that for teenagers it can feel weird to be located, but if she were in danger, it would help family find her faster and know exactly where she was.

Does the app give you more freedom?

Yes. She felt that being able to share location can make parents more comfortable giving teenagers freedom to go to school, travel, and move around more independently.

What feels different about checking because I am worried versus checking out of curiosity?

She said there is a big difference. If I am genuinely worried, that makes sense to her. But if a parent is checking just to check, especially all the time, then it starts to feel unnecessary.

When does protection become too much control?

Her answer was one of the strongest parts of the interview: when a parent keeps checking constantly, even after already seeing that their child is in the same safe place. For her, that is when it starts crossing the line.

Does Life360 mean I do not trust you?

No. She said she sees it more as a safety measure than a lack of trust. In her mind, the main purpose is making sure she is okay, not assuming she is doing something wrong.

If you could make rules for parents using Life360, what would they be?

Use it when there is an actual reason. Do not obsess over every stop. And if something looks confusing, ask first before assuming the worst.

What does trust from a parent mean to you?

She said trust means believing your child is being honest and responsible, while still caring about their safety. For her, trust and location sharing are not automatically opposites.

03 — Interview 2

Younger daughter (age 13)

Age 13

How do you feel knowing I can see your location?

She said it does not bother her in a major way because she knows why I use it. To her, it feels more like a safety backup than something negative by itself.

Does Life360 make you feel safer, watched, or both?

She also leaned toward both. She said it can be helpful, especially when traveling alone or taking transportation, but she would not want it to feel like someone is constantly following every move.

Do you think the app gives you more independence?

Yes. She felt that parents may allow more independence when they know they have another way to make sure their child got somewhere safely. In that sense, the app can create more freedom instead of less.

What is the difference between concern and curiosity?

She said checking because a parent is worried is understandable. But checking only out of curiosity, especially repeatedly, can feel like too much because there is no real reason behind it.

When does it start to feel controlling?

For her, it starts feeling controlling when parents want to question every stop, every delay, or every small change, even if nothing is actually wrong.

Do you think using Life360 means I do not trust you?

No. She said she sees it more as me wanting to know she is safe. She did not connect it directly with distrust unless the app is used in an extreme or constant way.

If parents should have rules for using the app, what would you say?

She felt parents should use it mainly for safety, respect privacy, and avoid reacting too quickly to every small thing they see on the screen.

If a friend asked you whether sharing location with parents is okay, what would you say?

She would say it can be okay if the relationship already has communication and the parent is not abusing the app. For her, the app itself is not the whole problem; it depends on how the parent uses it.

04 — What they shared

Where both interviews overlapped

What both agreed on

  • Location sharing can be useful for safety.
  • It can also feel uncomfortable if used too much.
  • It does not automatically mean a parent does not trust a child.
  • Parents should not check constantly just because they can.

What stood out to me

Both girls basically gave me the same message in different words: the app is not the real problem. The real issue is whether the parent uses it with boundaries, trust, and communication. That lined up almost perfectly with what my research suggested.

“
After hearing both of my daughters, I felt even more sure that the real question is not whether parents should ever use location-sharing apps. The real question is whether we know how to use them without turning safety into control.

— Cindy Williams Ureña
