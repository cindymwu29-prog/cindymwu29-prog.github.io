Their Voices

Edited interview material from both of my daughters

I wanted this page to feel more personal because one of the most important parts of this project was not only researching what scholars say, but also listening to the teenagers in my own home. I identify them only by age and avoid school names, routes, and other identifying details.

Interview 1: Age 15 Interview 2: Age 13 Topic: Life360, trust, safety, independence

01 — Interview note

I did not want to talk about teenagers without hearing from them directly.

For this project, I interviewed both of my daughters separately. I chose not to use their names, schools, routes, or any identifying details. I only refer to them by age. That was important to me because the project is public-facing, and I wanted to respect their privacy while still including their real perspectives.

The interviews are not scholarly evidence and I am not treating them that way. Instead, they help me compare academic research with two real teenagers living with the technology. The older interview is represented with a few direct phrases from the recording. The younger interview is presented mainly as careful paraphrase so I do not put quotation marks around wording that is not word-for-word.

02 — Interview 1

Older daughter (age 15)

Age 15

How do you feel knowing I can see your location?

She explained that knowing I can see her exact whereabouts makes her feel safer, especially if she were ever in danger and needed us to find her.

Does Life360 make you feel safer, watched, or both?

Her answer was clear: a little bit of both. She said that for teenagers it can feel weird to be located, but if she were in serious danger, family would be able to know where she was and make sure she was safe.

Does the app give you more freedom?

Yes. She agreed that being able to see her location makes me more comfortable giving her freedom to travel to school, go out, and use public transportation on her own.

What feels different about checking because I am worried versus checking out of curiosity?

She said that if I were checking only out of curiosity, I could simply text or call. She understood my checking the app more as something I would do when I am trying to make sure she is safe.

When does protection become too much control?

Her answer was immediate: when a parent checks all the time. She said that once you know your child has been safely in the same place for hours, there is no reason to keep watching the app twenty-four seven or monitoring every movement.

Does Life360 mean I do not trust you?

No. When I asked her to answer from her own experience, she said, I just feel like you're worried about my safety. That's it.

If you could make rules for parents using Life360, what would they be?

Her main rule was not to check regularly without a reason. If hours go by and something seems wrong, first try to contact the teenager. She also said parents need some trust in their child instead of becoming paranoid and checking constantly.

Does sharing location affect your independence?

She said no. To her, sharing location means she is safer and the people who care about her know where she is. She also agreed that because I feel safer, she can do more things independently.

Would you recommend location sharing to a friend?

Yes. She said teenagers should share their location with family because unexpected or dangerous things can happen in the street, at school, or while walking, and family may need to know where they are.

Can a parent trust a child and still want to know where they are?

Yes. She said trust gives a child freedom, but wanting to know a location can still be about safety. In her words, parents may trust their child but not necessarily every person or situation around them.

03 — Interview 2

Younger daughter (age 13)

Age 13
Paraphrased from the recorded interview

Her answers below are presented as careful paraphrases rather than quotation marks. I wanted to keep her meaning while avoiding pretending that a paraphrase is a word-for-word transcript.

How do you feel knowing I can see your location?

She said that knowing I can see where she is makes her feel safer. She connected the app with having a backup if something happens while she is away from home.

Does Life360 make you feel safer, watched, or both?

She emphasized the safety side more strongly than her older sister did. She understands that the app can show where she is, but she did not describe that as a problem when it is being used for a real safety reason.

Do you think the app gives you more freedom?

Yes. She explained that when I know she arrived safely or can find her if something goes wrong, I am more comfortable letting her do more things independently.

Is checking because I am worried different from checking out of curiosity?

Yes. She saw checking because something seems wrong as understandable. Checking repeatedly when nothing is wrong felt unnecessary to her.

When does protection start to feel controlling?

For her, the line is crossed when the app is used to follow every movement instead of as a safety tool. The reason for checking matters.

Does Life360 mean I do not trust you?

No. She connected my use of the app more with wanting to know that she is safe than with believing she is doing something wrong.

What rules should parents have for an app like Life360?

Her answers pointed toward using it when it is actually needed, not reacting to every small movement, and continuing to communicate directly instead of letting the app replace conversation.

Does sharing location hurt your independence?

She did not see it that way. In her experience, location sharing can actually support independence because it gives me enough reassurance to let her travel and do more on her own.

Would you recommend location sharing to another teenager?

She viewed sharing with trusted family members as useful for safety, especially when a teenager is traveling alone or using public transportation.

Can a parent trust a child and still want to know where they are?

Yes. Her interview supported the idea that trust in the teenager and concern about the situations around them can exist at the same time.

04 — What they shared

They agreed on the purpose, but not with exactly the same emphasis.

Where they agreed

  • Location sharing can be useful for safety.
  • It does not automatically mean a parent distrusts a teenager.
  • Parents should not monitor every movement just because the app makes it possible.
  • The app can support independence when it gives parents enough reassurance to let teenagers do more on their own.

Where their emphasis differed

My 15-year-old daughter spoke more openly about the tension of feeling both safer and a little watched. My 13-year-old daughter emphasized the safety and independence side more strongly. That difference mattered to me because it showed that even inside the same family, the same technology can be experienced a little differently.

“
Listening to both of them made me realize that the real boundary is not the location dot itself. It is what we do with it after we see it.
