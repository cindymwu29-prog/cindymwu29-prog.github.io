Safety, Trust, and Teenage Independence

Research Question

In what ways do location-sharing apps such as Life360 shape how parents of teenagers who commute to and from school independently balance safety, trust, and independence?

01 — Why this question became personal

Location sharing started as backup, not surveillance.

When my daughters started going to and from school on their own, location sharing did not feel like surveillance to me. It felt like backup. We already communicate through calls and messages, so Life360 was never supposed to replace conversation. Knowing that I could check where they were if something went wrong made me more comfortable giving them freedom to travel independently.

Still, the more I researched the topic, the more I realized that the same little dot on a map can mean very different things. For a parent, it can mean safety and peace of mind. For a teenager, it can also mean being watched. I do not think the app itself creates trust or destroys it. The bigger difference seems to be why parents check, how often they check, and what they do with the information.

02 — What the research says

The technology is common. The meaning depends on context.

Davis et al. (2024) reviewed research on digital location tracking and found estimates suggesting that 33% to 69% of U.S. families use it. What caught my attention is that they also describe this parenting behavior as vastly understudied. Families have adopted these tools faster than researchers have been able to fully understand their effects. That matters because adolescence is already a period when young people are asking for more privacy, freedom, and responsibility. A tool that gives parents almost constant access to a teenager’s location can support independence, but it can also create tension if freedom begins to feel connected to being watched.

Burnell et al. (2023) studied 729 adolescents, with an average age of about fifteen, and found that about half of parents and adolescents reported digital location tracking. Younger adolescents and girls were more likely to be tracked. Some associations between tracking and negative outcomes appeared, especially among older adolescents and adolescents who reported lower positive parenting, but the authors were careful to call the study preliminary and noted that the findings were not robust across every analysis. I think that caution is important. The study does not prove that tracking harms teenagers. It suggests that family context matters. A teenager who already feels supported may interpret location sharing differently from one who already feels controlled.

Langlais and Marich (2024) focused specifically on Life360. They surveyed 285 young adults, around age twenty on average, about how their parents used the app during high school and college. Their findings were mixed. Greater parental use was connected with parental involvement and connectedness, while lower self-esteem was associated with parents contacting their children because of something they saw on the app. Most other relationships were weak. I found that more useful than a simple positive or negative answer. It made me think that the problem may not be seeing the dot, but what happens after a parent sees it. Checking because a child is unexpectedly late is different from sending a message every time the dot moves.

03 — What teenagers say

The person being tracked has a voice too.

Family interview note

I interviewed my daughter separately and identify her only by age in this public project. Her comments are personal experience, not a replacement for scholarly research.

I wanted the teenage perspective because adults can easily discuss safety without asking how monitoring feels to the person being monitored. Dereymaeker et al. (2026) conducted 21 focus groups with 147 young people ages thirteen to sixteen in Belgium. The teenagers generally accepted location sharing when it involved close, trusted people, confirmed ordinary places such as school routes, and would remain non-continuous. That phrase stayed with me because it gives a simple boundary: location sharing can exist without becoming constant observation.

“
It makes me feel a little bit of both… at the same time you’re being located, which is kind of weird for a lot of teenagers, but if you are in really bad danger, they’ll be able to know your whereabouts and make sure you’re safe.

— 15-year-old daughter, family interview, October 2026

I heard something similar when I interviewed my fifteen-year-old daughter. I asked whether Life360 made her feel safer, watched, or a little of both. She answered that it was a little bit of both. She understood that being located can feel strange for teenagers, but she also said that if she were in danger, knowing her exact whereabouts could help us find her. When I asked when protection starts to feel like too much control, her answer was immediate: when a parent checks all the time. Once a parent already knows a child is safely in the same place, she said, there is no reason to keep watching every movement.

04 — Trust and independence are not opposites

Sometimes knowing where she is makes it easier for me to let go.

What surprised me most was that she did not connect my use of Life360 with a lack of trust. She sees it as concern for her safety. She also made a point I had been thinking about throughout the project: if a parent truly did not trust a teenager at all, that parent might not allow the teenager to travel independently in the first place. In our family, the app has actually made it easier for me to give more freedom because I know I have a backup if normal communication fails.

Her answer does not mean every teenager will feel the same way. Dereymaeker et al. (2026) show that young people negotiate their own boundaries, and Burnell et al. (2023) suggest that age and parenting context may affect how tracking is experienced. Good intentions are not enough by themselves. Parents also have to pay attention to how teenagers experience the technology.

05 — Where I draw the line

Access is not the same thing as permission to watch constantly.

After researching this topic, I would not use location sharing as a substitute for communication, and I would not check simply because the information is available. For me, the healthiest use is as backup: a teenager is unexpectedly late, transportation is delayed, a call or text goes unanswered, or there is a real safety concern. If checking becomes constant or leads to questions about every stop, the purpose can change from protection to control.

That distinction answers my research question more clearly than saying Life360 is good or bad. Location-sharing apps can help parents feel safe enough to give teenagers more independence, but only when trust and communication already exist. The technology can support freedom when it stays in the background. It can undermine freedom when it becomes the main way a parent knows what a teenager is doing.

06 — A simple symbol, a complicated meaning

One location marker can carry several meanings.

That is why I chose this simple image: the technology itself cannot tell us whether a family is practicing care, convenience, surveillance, or control. The relationship around the technology gives the dot its meaning.

07 — Conclusion

Knowing when not to look may be part of learning to trust.

Before this project, I mostly thought of Life360 as a practical safety tool. I still see that value, but now I think more about the boundary around its use. The research did not convince me to stop using location sharing. It made me more aware that access to information does not mean I need to use that information constantly. My daughter’s response also reminded me that teenagers can understand the safety reason while still wanting limits.

Maybe the real challenge is not having access to our teenagers’ location, but knowing when we do not need to look.
