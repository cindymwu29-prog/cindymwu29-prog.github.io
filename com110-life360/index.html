Multimodal Research Project

That Little Dot on the Map

Safety, Trust, and Teenage Independence

Research Question: In what ways do location-sharing apps such as Life360 shape how parents of teenagers who commute to and from school independently balance safety, trust, and independence?

Explore the project

01 — Why this question became personal

Location sharing started as backup, not surveillance.

When my daughters started traveling to and from school on their own, sharing their location never felt like surveillance to me. It felt like backup. We already communicate through calls and messages, so the app was not there to replace conversation. Knowing that I could check where they were if something went wrong simply made me more comfortable giving them freedom to move around independently.

But the more I researched the topic, the more I realized that the same little dot on a map can mean different things to different people. For a parent, it can mean safety and peace of mind. For a teenager, it can also mean being watched. That tension became the center of this project: not whether location-sharing technology is simply good or bad, but how families use it and where protection can begin to feel like control.

02 — What the research says

The technology is common. The meaning depends on context.

01

Location tracking is already part of many families’ routines.

Davis et al. (2024) reviewed research on digital location tracking of children and adolescents and reported estimates suggesting that roughly one-third to more than two-thirds of U.S. families use some form of location tracking. At the same time, they describe the field as still “vastly understudied.” That combination interested me because families are already using the technology while research is still catching up with what it may mean for development, privacy, relationships, and trust.

02

Teenagers do not all experience tracking the same way.

Burnell et al. (2023) studied 729 adolescents and found that digital location tracking was common among participating families. Their findings suggest that how teens experience tracking can vary with age and family context. This matters because checking a teenager’s location in an emergency is not the same behavior as monitoring every movement throughout the day. The tool may be identical, but the relationship around it is not.

03

Life360 can be connected to both involvement and pressure.

Langlais and Marich (2024) examined young adults’ recollections of parental Life360 use during high school and college. Their results were not simply positive or negative. Some use was associated with parental involvement and connectedness, while certain parent reactions to app activity were associated with less positive outcomes. That complexity supports one of the main ideas of this project: the issue may not be the dot on the map itself, but what a parent does with the information after seeing it.

04

Young people often accept location sharing when trust already exists.

Dereymaeker et al. (2026) conducted focus groups with 147 young people ages 13 to 16 in Belgium. Youth were more accepting of location sharing with trusted people and in ordinary situations such as school routes, especially when tracking would “remain non-continuous.” Their work adds an important teenage perspective: location sharing may feel acceptable when it has a clear purpose and boundaries, but continuous observation can change its meaning.

03 — Safety or surveillance?

The line may be frequency, intention, and communication.

SAFETYbackup • emergencies • reassurance
TRUST + COMMUNICATIONhow often • why • what happens next
CONTROLconstant checking • questioning • pressure

Research moved me away from the idea that an app can be judged by itself. A parent may open Life360 because a child is unexpectedly late, a train is delayed, or a call has gone unanswered. That is very different from watching the map throughout the day and questioning every stop. Both behaviors technically use the same feature, but they communicate different messages. One says, “I need a backup if normal communication fails.” The other can begin to say, “I need to verify everything you do.” That difference is where safety, trust, and independence meet.

04 — What teenagers actually think

Real-life material complicated the question in a useful way.

To compare scholarly research with everyday family experience, I interviewed my daughters separately. In this public project I identify them only by age. Their answers are not intended to replace scholarly research. Instead, they offer real-life perspectives that can support, question, or complicate what the studies suggest.

“
It makes me feel a little bit of both… at the same time you’re being located, which is kind of weird for a lot of teenagers, but if you are in really bad danger, they’ll be able to know your whereabouts and make sure you’re safe.

— 15-year-old daughter, family interview

When does protection become too much control?

Her answer was simple: when a parent checks “all the time.” She explained that if a teenager has been in the same location for hours, a parent already knows where that child is. In her view, the app makes more sense when it is checked for an actual safety reason instead of out of constant curiosity.

Does tracking automatically mean a lack of trust?

She did not experience it that way. She described my use of Life360 as concern for her safety rather than proof that I do not trust her. She also connected trust with freedom: if parents had no trust at all, they might not allow a teenager to go out independently in the first place.

Can location sharing actually support independence?

Her answer was yes. She said sharing her location does not take away her independence because she still travels and does things on her own. From her perspective, location sharing provides another layer of safety while allowing that independence to continue.

05 — One app, two perspectives

Parents and teenagers may look at the same feature differently.

A parent may see…

  • Safety
  • Peace of mind
  • Emergency backup
  • Confidence to allow more freedom
  • A way to notice that something may be wrong

A teenager may feel…

  • Safety
  • Reassurance
  • Being protected
  • Being watched if checking becomes constant
  • Too much control if every movement is questioned

The technology may be the same. What changes is how it is used.

07 — Finding the line

Trust does not require parents to know nothing. It requires knowing when information is enough.

My research does not lead me to conclude that Life360 or similar apps are simply good or bad. They can help parents feel safe enough to give teenagers more independence, especially when teens travel to school or use public transportation alone. But that benefit depends on the family relationship around the technology. Communication must come first. Tracking should be a backup, not the main way a parent relates to a teenager.

The research and my first family interview also point in the same direction: teenagers may accept location sharing when they understand why it is being used, trust already exists, and monitoring is not constant. That does not mean every teenager will feel the same way, and it does not mean every parent will use the technology responsibly. It means the most important questions may be how often parents check, what they do with the information, and whether teenagers still feel trusted to make decisions on their own.

Maybe the real challenge is not having access to our teenagers’ location, but knowing when we do not need to look.
